Title: Structurally-Grounded Automated Literature Review Generation via
Citation-Graph-Guided Section PlanningProblem: Generating a coherent,
comprehensive, and correctly cited literature survey from a corpus of papers is
an open challenge. Existing approaches (e.g., extractive summarization, naive
retrieval-augmented generation) produce reviews that either (a) list papers
sequentially without organizing them into a narrative structure, (b) hallucinate
citations or misattribute claims, or (c) fail to cover the breadth of a field
because they lack explicit awareness of how papers relate to one another
(support, contradict, extend, apply). A literature review is fundamentally a
structured argument about the state of a field, not a compressed document.
Current methods treat structure as a post-hoc decoration rather than a
first-class planning objective.

Hypothesis: If a system first constructs a citation graph over the input paper
set, extracts structural roles (seminal work, baseline, improvement,
application, rebuttal) and relationship types (extends, contradicts, reproduces,
benchmarks) from citation contexts, and uses these to plan
hierarchically-organized sections before generating prose, then the resulting
literature review will achieve higher citation-fidelity, better topical
coverage, and more coherent narrative flow than systems that generate sections
either sequentially or from flat retrieval.

Method: We propose a three-stage pipeline:

 1 Citation-Graph Construction & Role Assignment. Given a query topic and a seed
   set of papers (from the initial corpus), construct a directed citation graph.
   For each edge, extract the citing sentence and classify the relationship into
   one of five categories using a fine-tuned sentence encoder: extends,
   contradicts, reproduces, benchmarks-against, or unrelated. Simultaneously,
   assign each paper a structural role (seminal, baseline, state-of-the-art,
   survey, application, tool/dataset) based on its citation profile (in-degree,
   recency, h-index of venue) and abstract content.
 2 Hierarchical Section Planning. Use a planner (e.g., an LLM prompted with
   graph statistics or a lightweight learned policy over section templates) to
   produce a two-level outline. At level1, sections correspond to topic clusters
   extracted from the citation graph via community detection (e.g., Louvain on
   co-citation edges). At level2, within each section, papers are ordered by a
   combination of role priority (seminal → baseline → SotA → extension) and
   chronological flow. Each section's plan specifies: (a) which papers to cite,
   (b) what relationship narrative to use (e.g., "X extended Y by..."), and (c)
   a one-sentence section thesis summarizing the contribution of that group of
   papers to the field.
 3 Controlled Generation with Citation-Grounded Decoding. Generate section text
   section-by-section using an LLM prompted with (i) the section thesis, (ii)
   the ordered paper list with extracted relationship sentences, and (iii) the
   full abstract of each paper. To reduce hallucination, we apply a
   citation-grounded decoding constraint: after each generated sentence, the
   model must select at least one citation from the paper list for that section;
   generation stops per section when all planned citations for that section have
   been used or the section thesis has been fully addressed. No cross-section
   citations are permitted unless a paper appears in two planned sections (which
   is allowed with a cross-reference marker).

Dataset / Benchmark: We use the SciReviewGen corpus (introduced by Luu et
al.,2024), which pairs query topics with full gold-standard survey papers and
their reference lists. We filter for topics where at least15–25 papers in the
gold survey are available as full text (via S2ORC or unpaywall). This yields
approximately80–120 evaluation topics across computer science subfields. Each
gold review is split into sections at the level-2 heading depth.

Evaluation Metrics:

 • ROUGE-1/2/L and BLEU for n-gram overlap with gold reviews (standard measure,
   known limitations).
 • Citation-F1 (proposed): the F1 score over the set of cited papers per
   section, compared against the gold review's reference list per section.
   Penalizes hallucinated citations and missing required citations.
 • Narrative Coherence (proposed): human evaluation (5-point Likert) of logical
   flow, section-to-section transitions, and thesis clarity, rated by at least3
   annotators per example on30 randomly sampled topics.
 • Factual Consistency: measured via DeBERTa-based NLI (entailment rate between
   generated claims and source abstracts), averaged across all sections.
 • Coverage: fraction of gold-reference papers that appear in the generated
   review at least once.

Baselines:

 1 Naive RAG: retrieve top-10 papers per query, generate a flat list-style
   summary via GPT-4o (no structure planning).
 2 Sequential Section Generation: section-level retrieval (K=5 papers per
   section) with GPT-4o, sections generated in order with no cross-section
   planning (Gidiotis & Tsoumakas,2023).
 3 Topic-Model-Guided: LDA on all paper abstracts → topic clusters → generate
   one paragraph per topic → concatenate (standard text mining baseline).
 4 Our Method (Full): the full pipeline described above.
 5 Ablation: No Graph Roles: same pipeline but skip role assignment — use
   community detection only, no relationship-edge labels.

Expected failure modes and how they are handled:

 • Fragmentary output. If community detection produces too many small clusters
   (e.g.,20+ for25 papers), sections become too narrow. Mitigation: merge
   clusters smaller than3 papers into the nearest large cluster.
 • Citation assignment errors. Classifying "extends" vs "benchmarks-against" is
   ambiguous in dense subfields (e.g., LLM evaluation). Mitigation: use a
   confidence threshold (p ≥0.65); below threshold, default to "unrelated" and
   omit the edge from planning.
 • Thesis drift during generation. The LLM may generate text that wanders from
   the section thesis. Mitigation: include the section thesis as a fixed prefix
   prompt term; after the draft, run an entailment classifier between generated
   sentences and the thesis; flag and regenerate sentences that fall below a0.5
   entailment threshold.

Limitations:

 • The method depends on the availability of a reasonably complete citation
   graph. Fields with sparse citation practices or small paper counts (<10) may
   yield poor results.
 • Citation roles are assigned heuristically; a paper's actual contribution may
   not match its citation-profile-based role (e.g., a highly-cited paper that is
   primarily a method survey may be misclassified as "seminal").
 • The generation step uses a closed-source LLM (GPT-4o), introducing
   reproducibility concerns and potential API-level drift over time. All
   experiments will log exact model, temperature (0.3 for all), and seed
   parameters.
 • Human evaluation for narrative coherence is expensive and covers only a
   subset of topics. We will report inter-annotator agreement (Krippendorff's α)
   and release annotation guidelines.
 • The citation-grounded decoding constraint is a soft heuristic (prompt-level
   instruction + post-hoc filtering), not a trained decoding objective, and may
   occasionally be ignored by the model despite the prompt.
