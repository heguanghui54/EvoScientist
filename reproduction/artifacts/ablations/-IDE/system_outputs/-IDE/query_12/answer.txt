     Atomic Factual Consistency via Fine-grained Preference Optimization##
    ProblemLarge Language Models (LLMs) generate fluent, coherent text that
routinely contains factually incorrect atomic claims. Current alignment methods
 — RLHF, DPO, and variants — rely on holistic reward models or preference pairs
    that score the overall quality of a generation. This coarse signal masks
 fine-grained factual errors: a paragraph may be informative, well-structured,
  and compelling while still containing two or three false atomic claims. The
reward model's capacity to detect individual false claims is limited by both the
     granularity of the reward signal and the sparsity of factual errors in
 preference data. As a result, aligned models still hallucinate at non-trivial
    rates, especially on knowledge-intensive and long-form generation tasks.

HypothesisTraining a language model to prefer generations in which every atomic
claim is individually verifiable against a trusted knowledge corpus will
significantly reduce factual inconsistency without materially sacrificing
fluency, informativeness, or coverage. More precisely: if we decompose each
generation into atomic claims, assign each claim a fine-grained consistency
score against retrieved evidence via bidirectional natural language inference,
and optimize the model to maximize its worst-claim score, then the resulting
model will be both factually more reliable and better calibrated about the
boundary of its knowledge.

MethodThe method has four components:

1. Atomic Claim Extraction. Fine-tune a lightweight sequence tagger (e.g.,
DeBERTa-v3-base) on a synthetic dataset of model outputs annotated with atomic
claims using distant supervision from semantic role labeling and constituency
parsing. Each atomic claim is a single factual proposition that is either true
or false (e.g., "The Eiffel Tower was completed in1889"). Claims can be explicit
or entailed by the text. The extractor operates on the model's own generated
text at inference time.

2. Multi-source Factual Verification. For each extracted atomic claim, retrieve
the top-5 most relevant passages from a static trusted knowledge corpus (a2024
snapshot of Wikipedia). A bidirectional NLI model (T5-XXL fine-tuned on MNLI +
FEVER + Vitaminc + ANLI) predicts three probabilities: entailment (the passage
supports the claim), contradiction (the passage refutes the claim), and neutral
(the passage is relevant but does not confirm or refute). The per-claim
consistency score is defined as P(entailment) — P(contradiction), bounded in
[-1,1]. Claims for which retrieval returns no relevant passages are marked as
unverified and assigned a penalty score of -0.5.

3. Worst-C claim Aggregation. Given a generation y containing k atomic claims
with scores s₁, …, sₖ, the fine-grained consistency reward is R(y) = min(s₁, …,
sₖ). Using the minimum (rather than the mean) forces the model to address its
weakest factual failures and prevents it from trading a few bad claims against
many good ones. Unverified claims are included in the min, making the model
conservative when it lacks relevant knowledge.

4. Fine-grained Preference Optimization. Construct preference pairs from the
model's own generations on a diverse set of prompts. For each prompt, sample two
outputs: (a) the standard greedy decode, and (b) a contrastively decoded output
obtained by subtracting low-consistency hidden states from high-consistency
hidden states at each layer (inspired by contrastive decoding but using the
NLI-based consistency signal instead of a small/large model gap). Compute the
consistency reward for both. Apply DPO on the resulting pairs, where the
higher-reward output is the preferred completion. Train for3 epochs with a
held-out validation set for early stopping.

Training prompts are drawn from the Pile, Natural Questions, and ELI5. The
trusted knowledge corpus for verification is Wikipedia (2024-06-01 dump,
pre-processed into100-token passages with a dense retriever index).

Evaluation Metrics- Primary: Factual precision (fraction of atomic claims that
are entailed by the knowledge corpus) on LongFact. This directly measures the
central claim.

 • Secondary: TruthfulQA accuracy, FELQA FactScore, FACTOR macro F1.
 • Fluency controls: Perplexity on a held-out WikiText-103 set, MAUVE score
   against human-written text, and human preference rate (fluency &
   informativeness) via a small-scale crowd eval.
 • Calibration: Expected Calibration Error on per-token factual confidence
   (internal consistency vs. verification outcome).

Expected Failure Modes1. Extraction noise. Atomic claims that are implicit,
nested, or span multiple sentences will be missed or fragmented by the
extractor, producing incomplete coverage. Some factual errors will escape the
pipeline entirely. This is partially mitigated by the extractor's
recall-oriented training (optimize for F1 with recall weight0.7), but implicit
claims (e.g., "She won, which surprised everyone" — entails "A female individual
won a competition.") remain a hard open problem.

 2 Verifier compounding. The T5-XXL NLI model has its own factual and
   consistency blind spots. If it systematically misses a class of
   contradictions (e.g., temporal reasoning or numeric comparisons), the reward
   model will be blind to them and the optimization may even reinforce them. We
   will analyze verifier failure modes by stress-testing on FEVER2.0 adversarial
   examples before training.
 3 Vacuity — the conservatism trap. The worst-claim reward strongly penalizes
   unverifiable claims. The model may learn to produce text that avoids making
   factual claims altogether — vague hedging, meta-commentary, or trivial
   restatements of the prompt — to maximize the reward. We guard against this by
   (a) including a coverage penalty in the reward (the fraction of tokens that
   participate in at least one atomic claim), and (b) monitoring MAUVE and human
   informativeness ratings as fluency controls.
 4 Ambiguous reference resolution. Retrieval cannot correctly verify claims that
   depend on unresolved pronouns or vague noun phrases (e.g., "This finding
   contradicts previous work" — which finding? which previous work?). The atomic
   extractor will attempt to ground references via coreference resolution, but
   failures here produce unverifiable claims, triggering the penalty and
   reducing the model's willingness to make grounded comparisons.

Limitations- Corpus-bound knowledge. The method only verifies claims against the
static Wikipedia corpus. Claims about private facts, unreported events,
proprietary data, or knowledge that post-dates the corpus will be penalized as
unverifiable. This creates an inherent bias toward well-documented public
knowledge and away from novelty. Extending to live retrieval from web sources or
API-based knowledge bases is left for future work.

 • Definitions of "factual." The method operationalizes factual consistency as
   entailment by a trusted corpus. This conflates factual correctness with
   corpus coverage and does not address factual claims that are true but
   unsupported by the specific corpus (e.g., newly published research). It also
   cannot handle normative, subjective, or predictive claims, which are not
   factual in the verification sense.
 • Pipeline complexity. The system chains an extractor, a retriever, a verifier,
   and a preference optimiser. Errors compound. Inference-time latency increases
   by200–500 ms per generation (dominated by the verifier). A single-model
   end-to-end alternative (e.g., self-verification) may be preferable in
   latency-critical settings.
 • Scope of hallucination. The method targets factual hallucinations — claims
   that contradict verifiable knowledge. It does not address
   instruction-following errors, logical fallacies, stylistic inconsistency, or
   structural hallucinations (e.g., incorrect graph topology in a generated
   table). A complete solution likely requires combining factual verification
   with behaviorally grounded alignment signals.
 • Scalability of verification. Scoring every atomic claim from every training
   generation is expensive. For50k training prompts with average8 claims per
   generation, verification requires ~400k T5-XXL forward passes per epoch. We
   mitigate this by caching retrieval results and using LoRA adapters for the
   verifier, but full-scale training remains GPU-intensive and may require
   off-policy distillation to amortize the cost.
