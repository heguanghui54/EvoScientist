Cross-Lingual Lexical Bootstrapping for Low-Resource Neural Machine Translation
via Cognate-Guided Code-Switching### ProblemNeural machine translation (NMT)
systems require large parallel corpora to achieve acceptable quality. For the
vast majority of the world's languages — those with fewer than1 million speakers
and minimal digitized text — parallel data is scarce or nonexistent. Existing
low-resource approaches fall into three categories: transfer learning from a
high-resource parent language, unsupervised methods that rely on monolingual
corpora and back-translation, and data augmentation using lexical substitution.
Each has a known ceiling: transfer learning degrades sharply when the parent and
target are typologically distant; unsupervised methods produce fluent but
semantically brittle output; and lexical augmentation methods are limited by the
coverage of existing bilingual lexicons. No current method systematically
exploits the phonological and orthographic signal shared between related
languages — cognates — to generate synthetic parallel data at the sentence
level, then uses that data to train a joint encoder that generalizes beyond the
lexical overlap.

HypothesisH1. Incorporating cognate-guided code-switching as a data augmentation
strategy injects sufficient parallel signal into low-resource MT training to
improve BLEU by ≥3 points over back-translation-only baselines at
the10k-sentence parallel data regime.

H2. A shared bilingual encoder pretrained on a cognate-detection objective
learns cross-lingual representations that transfer better to the translation
task than standard cross-lingual language model pretraining (e.g., XLM-R)
because the objective forces alignment at the subword level rather than the
sentence level.

H3. The gains are largest for language pairs with moderate cognate density
(15–35% lexical overlap) and diminish at both very high overlap (where standard
transfer already works) and very low overlap (where the signal from cognates is
too sparse). This produces an identifiable U-shaped utility curve, which can be
used to decide a priori whether cognate bootstrapping will help a given language
pair.

MethodThe proposed method, CognateBoot, proceeds in four stages:

Stage1: Cognate Detection and Lexicon Induction. Given a source language L_s and
target language L_t with a small seed parallel lexicon (500–2000 entries), train
a cognate classifier that operates on subword sequences. The classifier uses
edit distance over phonemized forms (via the Epitran grapheme-to-phoneme
library) combined with a learned alignment score from a2-layer Transformer over
character n-grams. This detects cognates even when orthographic divergence is
high (e.g., Spanish noche → Italian notte). Output: a bilingual cognate lexicon
of size k (target:5,000–20,000 pairs, depending on language pair).

Stage2: Cognate-Guided Code-Switching Augmentation. For each sentence in the
target-side monolingual corpus (which is assumed to be larger than the parallel
data, e.g.,100k–500k sentences), identify content words that have a known
cognate in L_s via the induced lexicon. Replace each such word with its cognate
equivalent with probability p =0.3 (tuned on a held-out development set). Apply
a syntactic constraint: only replace nouns, verbs, and adjectives; function
words and inflected forms are skipped unless the cognate match is exact in lemma
form. The result is a code-switched sentence that mixes L_t content with L_s
lexical insertions, creating a synthetic parallel-like signal without requiring
a full translation model.

Stage3: Joint Encoder Pretraining on Cognate Detection. Pretrain a6-layer
Transformer encoder on a binary classification task: given a pair of sentences
(s_i, t_j) — one from L_s, one from L_t — predict whether they are translations.
The positive pairs come from (a) the seed parallel corpus, (b) synthetic
translation pairs where t_j is the code-switched version of s_i, and (c)
back-translated pairs from a preliminary unsupervised NMT model. Negative pairs
are sampled from mismatched corpora. This is a scaled-down version of the XLM
translation-pair pretraining objective, but with two key differences: (i) the
training set is heavily weighted toward cognate-rich sentences (oversampled3×),
and (ii) the encoder is shallow (6 layers vs.12–24 in XLM-R) to match
low-resource compute budgets.

Stage4: Finetuning with Downsampling Curriculum. Finetune the pretrained
encoder–decoder on the seed parallel corpus (10k sentences) using a curriculum
that sorts training examples by cognate density from high to low. The intuition:
early training on cognate-dense examples anchors the cross-lingual mappings, and
later examples with fewer cognates force generalization beyond lexical overlap.
Decoding uses beam search with length penalty α =0.6. All experiments use
fairseq with a single GPU (12 GB VRAM).

Justification. These pairs cover moderate to high cognate density within a
single family branch. Spanish–Catalan tests near the upper bound of expected
utility; Hindi–Bengali tests the middle region where the method should produce
maximum relative gain; Indonesian–Malay tests a pair with high lexical overlap
but low orthographic convergence (both use Latin script, but phoneme-to-grapheme
mappings differ). An additional low-density pair (Swahili → Zulu, ~8% detectable
cognate rate) is included to test the lower bound of the U-shaped hypothesis.

Expected Failure Modes1. Cognate detection error propagates. If the induced
cognate lexicon has high precision but low recall (the expected failure mode of
edit-distance-based detection), the augmentation signal will be sparse. A recall
<10% would make Stage2 indistinguishable from random lexical substitution.
Mitigation: if Stage1 recall is below15% on a held-out cognate gold set, fall
back to a dictionary-based method (A1 configuration) and flag the result as a
non-cognate-driven outcome.

 2 Code-switching degrades target-language fluency. Replacing content words with
   source-language cognates may produce sentences that are locally
   understandable but whose global syntactic structure is corrupted (e.g.,
   verb-argument order mismatches between L_s and L_t in the Hindi–Bengali pair,
   where word order is more flexible). Mitigation: the syntactic constraint
   (Stage2) blocks function-word replacement; if LM perplexity on augmented
   sentences exceeds1.5× the perplexity on natural text, reduce p from0.3
   to0.15.
 3 Pretrained encoder overfits to cognate-detection proxy. The Stage3 binary
   classification task may produce an encoder that learns shallow orthographic
   cues (e.g., "if both sentences share the character sequence 'tion', predict
   positive") rather than deeper cross-lingual alignment. Mitigation: evaluate
   the pretrained encoder on the XL-Speech sentence retrieval task after Stage3;
   if recall@1 <20%, add a masked language modeling objective (λ =0.3) as
   regularization.
 4 Curriculum training causes catastrophic forgetting. If early
   high-cognate-density examples over-represent one domain (e.g., news text),
   the model may forget later low-cognate examples from other domains.
   Mitigation: monitor per-domain BLEU during finetuning; if a domain regresses
   by ≥2 BLEU relative to its starting point, revert to uniform shuffling.
 5 U-shaped curve is not observed — cognate density does not predict gain
   magnitude. This would falsify H3 and suggest that typological similarity
   (rather than lexical overlap per se) drives the improvement. If H3 fails, the
   paper will report the negative result and argue for a broader typological
   feature analysis instead.

Limitations1. Gold cognate lexicons are unavailable for most language pairs. The
evaluation relies on automatic cognate detection, which is itself an open
research problem for distantly related languages. Results on Spanish→Catalan may
overestimate performance on pairs with no standardized orthography.

 2 Monolingual corpus size is assumed (200k sentences). For languages with truly
   minimal digitization (e.g., <50k sentences total), the code-switching stage
   produces too few augmented sentences to affect training. The method is
   designed for languages with moderate digital presence (Wikipedia-sized or
   larger) rather than "extreme" low-resource languages (<10k sentences).
 3 No morphological handling. CognateBoot treats each word as an atomic unit. In
   morphologically rich languages (e.g., Bengali, Swahili), a single lemma may
   have dozens of surface forms, many of which will not be recognized by the
   edit-distance-based cognate detector. This systematically underestimates the
   true cognate density for inflected forms. A future extension would
   incorporate morphological segmentation (e.g., BPE over morphemes).
 4 Single GPU constraint limits encoder depth. The6-layer encoder may be too
   shallow to capture the cross-lingual abstraction that cognate-rich
   pretraining should provide, particularly for the Hindi→Bengali pair where
   script divergence adds visual noise. Results with a deeper encoder (12
   layers) on a subset of the data are reported as a secondary analysis but are
   not the primary claim.
 5 Evaluation languages are all from the same macro-family (Indo-European +
   Austronesian). The typological range is limited. Claims about
   generalizability to unrelated language families (Niger-Congo, Sino-Tibetan,
   Pama-Nyungan) are speculative until tested.
 6 Negative result risk on Swahili→Zulu. If the low cognate density (~8%)
   produces no measurable gain over back-translation, the U-shaped hypothesis is
   not fully tested (it requires a point near zero gain, not negative gain). A
   zero-result on this pair would be inconclusive rather than confirming the
   lower bound.
