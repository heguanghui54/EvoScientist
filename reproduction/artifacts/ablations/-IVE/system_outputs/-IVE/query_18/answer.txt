Research Proposal: User Experience Evaluation for Large Language Models## TitleA
   Multi-Dimensional Framework for Evaluating User Experience in Open-Domain
Conversational LLMs## ProblemLarge language models are increasingly deployed as
   conversational agents in open-domain settings, yet the dominant evaluation
  paradigm remains heavily skewed toward automated metrics (perplexity, BLEU,
ROUGE, METEOR, BERTScore, GPT-as-judge) and narrow benchmark performance (MMLU,
 GSM8K, HumanEval). These metrics capture surface-level linguistic fidelity and
  isolated capability dimensions but fail to measure how real users experience
   these systems over sustained interactions. User experience — encompassing
     satisfaction, trust, perceived intelligence, conversational flow, task
 completion comfort, and emotional response — remains under-characterized as a
       construct, with no standardized evaluation framework that is both
    psychometrically validated and practical for LLM developers. Existing UX
   instruments borrowed from HCI (SUS, QUIS, UEQ) were designed for graphical
interfaces and GUI applications, not for open-ended language interactions where
   the "interface" is entirely linguistic, context-dependent, and dynamically
                        co-constructed by both parties.

HypothesisA multi-dimensional UX evaluation framework designed specifically for
conversational LLMs — comprising (1) interaction fluency, (2) epistemic trust,
(3) goal alignment, (4) emotional valence, and (5) perceived adaptability — will
yield higher discriminative power, test-retest reliability, and predictive
validity for downstream behavioral outcomes (e.g., retention, referral, paid
conversion) than both:

 • H1a: Existing general-purpose UX instruments (SUS, UEQ) adapted post-hoc to
   conversational AI.
 • H1b: Single-metric LLM-as-judge evaluations currently prevalent in NLP.

Specifically, the framework will explain at least40% more variance in user
retention at2-week follow-up compared to the best existing alternative
(UEQ:12–18% variance explained in prior studies).

MethodPhase1 — Instrument Development (Qualitative → Quantitative). Conduct
semi-structured interviews with30 diverse LLM users (varying in technical
proficiency, use case, and interaction frequency). Thematic analysis will
surface the latent dimensions of user experience unique to conversational LLMs.
These dimensions will be operationalized into a25–30 item instrument using
Likert-scale and semantic-differential items. Items will be refined through
cognitive interviewing (n=10) and pilot testing (n=100) for clarity,
non-redundancy, and floor/ceiling effects.

Phase2 — Psychometric Validation (n=500). Deploy the instrument via a
longitudinal study. Participants interact with three different LLMs (two
instruction-tuned open-source models and one proprietary API) across five
scripted tasks (creative writing, factual question answering, emotional support,
code generation, multi-turn planning) over three sessions each. After each
session, participants complete the proposed instrument plus SUS, UEQ, and a
single-item GPT-as-judge correlation measure (participants rate "how intelligent
the model seemed"). At session3, participants complete a behavioral choice: they
may return for a follow-up session, recommend the model to a friend (binary), or
switch to a different model for the final task. Two-week follow-up surveys
measure retention and self-reported usage frequency.

Phase3 — Construct Validation (Discriminant and Convergent). Factor analysis
(EFA → CFA) assesses dimensionality. Correlate instrument dimensions with
automated metrics (perplexity, response length, self-repetition rate, lexical
diversity, BERTScore against ground-truth responses) to determine what automated
metrics capture (convergent) and miss (discriminant). Regress behavioral
outcomes (retention, recommendation) on instrument dimensions to establish
predictive validity.

Dataset / BenchmarkNo single existing dataset covers multi-model, multi-session
UX evaluation. The study will produce a new benchmark — LLM-UX-Bench —
comprising:

 • 500 participants ×3 models ×5 tasks ×3 sessions =22,500 interaction logs
   (anonymized, de-identified)
 • Item-level responses for the proposed instrument, SUS, UEQ, and the
   single-item intelligence rating- Behavioral outcomes: binary retention
   (returned for follow-up), binary recommendation willingness, continuous
   self-reported usage frequency at2 weeks- Model response transcripts (for
   reproducibility and automated metric computation)

Models under evaluation: Llama-3-70B-Instruct (open-source),
Mistral-8x22B-Instruct (open-source), GPT-4o (proprietary).

Evaluation MetricsPrimary instrument quality metrics:

 • Cronbach's α ≥0.80 per subscale (internal consistency)
 • Test-retest ICC ≥0.70 over1-week interval (stability)
 • Confirmatory Fit Index (CFI) ≥0.90, RMSEA ≤0.08 (structural validity)
 • Discriminant validity: average variance extracted (AVE) ≥0.50 per factor,
   inter-factor correlations ≤0.85Predictive validity metrics:
 • ΔR² in binary retention (logistic regression) and continuous usage (linear
   regression) compared to SUS, UEQ, and single-item intelligence rating- AUC
   for retention prediction (proposed instrument vs. alternatives)
 • Correlation matrix between instrument dimensions and automated NLG
   metricsComparison with automated evaluation:
 • Spearman correlation between each instrument dimension and GPT-as-judge
   scores (expected to be weak for emotional valence and trust dimensions,
   moderate for fluency)

Baselines1. System Usability Scale (SUS) — the most widely used general UX
instrument, adapted by rewording "system" to "conversational AI agent".

 2 User Experience Questionnaire (UEQ) —26 items across6 scales (Attractiveness,
   Perspicuity, Efficiency, Dependability, Stimulation, Novelty). Standard
   adaptation to conversational AI context.
 3 Single-item LLM Intelligence Rating — "On a scale of1–7, how intelligent did
   the model seem?" Matches the common "LLM-as-judge" paradigm but from the
   user's perspective.
 4 Automated NLG Metrics — Perplexity, BERTScore, Repetition Rate, Response
   Length, Distinct-N. Included to benchmark how much UX variance automated
   metrics already capture.

Ablations1. -TRUST: Omit the epistemic trust dimension from the instrument.
Measure loss in predictive power for retention and recommendation outcomes.
Tests whether trust is a separable and necessary construct or merely a correlate
of fluency.

 2 -EMOTION: Omit the emotional valence dimension. Tests whether affective
   response adds variance beyond cognitive appraisals (fluency, goal alignment).
   If predictive power does not drop, emotional valence may be redundant with
   satisfaction.
 3 -SINGLE-SESSION: Train and evaluate the predictive model on data from
   session1 only (no multi-session learning). Tests whether UX evaluations
   converge in a single interaction or require repeated exposure to stabilize.
 4 -BEHAVIORAL-ANCHORS: Replace behavioral-choice outcomes with
   hypothetical-intention items (e.g., "I would use this model again") as the
   dependent variable. Tests whether stated intentions predict actual behavior
   or are inflated by social desirability bias.

Expected Failure Modes1. Dimension collapse: Users may not distinguish between
perceived intelligence, trust, and satisfaction in practice (all load onto a
single "liking" factor). If EFA yields a single factor, the framework collapses
to a univariate measure and loses its claimed advantage over UEQ. Mitigation: if
this occurs, conduct a forced bifactor model to examine whether a general factor
dominates while specific factors still capture meaningful residual variance.

 2 Sequence effects dominate: Users' ratings in session2 may be driven primarily
   by contrast with the previous model rather than absolute experience. This
   would inflate variance and reduce test-retest reliability. Mitigation:
   counterbalance model order across participants and include order as a
   covariate in all regression models.
 3 Low retention base rate: If <20% of participants return for the2-week
   follow-up, retention becomes a near-constant with insufficient variation for
   logistic regression. Mitigation: oversample in the initial recruitment;
   target n=800 to account for35–40% attrition; pre-register a secondary
   continuous outcome (self-reported usage hours/week) as backup.
 4 Automated metrics already saturate UX prediction: If perplexity + BERTScore +
   repetition rate alone explain >50% of UX variance, the proposed instrument
   adds marginal value. This would itself be a significant negative result but
   weakens the paper's contribution. Mitigation: include ablation -AUTO that
   removes all automated metrics and tests whether the pure-instrument model
   still predicts retention.
 5 Social desirability bias inflates all self-report measures: Users may
   systematically over-report satisfaction for socially acceptable models (e.g.,
   GPT-4o, due to brand familiarity). This could create an artificial separation
   between proprietary and open-source models that reflects brand halo effects
   rather than UX. Mitigation: include a social desirability scale
   (Marlowe-Crowne short form) as a covariate; pre-register a sensitivity
   analysis without the proprietary model.
 6 Task heterogeneity obscures general factors: UX dimensions may factor
   differently by task type (emotional support → trust dominates; code
   generation → goal alignment dominates). If factor structure is non-invariant
   across tasks, a single unified instrument is invalid. Mitigation: test
   measurement invariance (configural, metric, scalar) across task types before
   pooling; if invariance fails, report task-specific subscales.

Limitations1. Self-selection bias: Participants who volunteer for a
multi-session LLM study are likely more tech-literate and positively disposed
toward AI than the general population. This limits generalizability to less
engaged or more skeptical user segments.

 2 Western-cultural instrument bias: Interview and validation samples will be
   predominantly English-speaking, likely drawn from MTurk/Prolific pools (US/UK
   demos). Emotional expression, trust calibration, and conversational norms
   vary significantly across cultures; the resulting instrument may not be
   cross-culturally invariant without substantial adaptation.
 3 Model obsolescence: The three target models represent a snapshot from
   early2025. As capabilities advance at a rapid pace, absolute UX scores will
   shift, and dimensions that matter today (e.g., factual accuracy concern) may
   become less salient while new dimensions (e.g., personalization depth,
   long-term memory) emerge. The framework's dimensionality should be
   re-validated annually.
 4 No ground truth for UX: Unlike classification accuracy, there is no objective
   "correct" UX score. All validation is necessarily relative (predicting
   downstream behavior). Behavioral outcomes (retention, referral) are
   themselves confounded by model pricing, API latency, brand preference, and
   contextual factors outside the interaction. The framework predicts user
   behavior, not an objective ground truth.
 5 Controlled tasks vs. naturalistic use: The five scripted tasks constrain
   interaction in ways that may not reflect how users actually engage with LLMs.
   Real-world use is self-directed, often interleaved with other activities, and
   varies in goal specificity. Ecological validity is traded for experimental
   control and cross-model comparability.
 6 No longitudinal calibration beyond2 weeks: Retention at2 weeks captures
   early-adoption patterns but does not measure long-term habituation,
   disenchantment, or skill development (users learning to prompt better over
   time). A longer horizon (3–6 months) may reveal different UX dynamics not
   captured here.
