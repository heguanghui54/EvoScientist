Title: Fair, Calibrated, and Distribution-Aware AI-Generated Text Detection: A
Multi-Stage Framework for False Positive MitigationProblem

Commercial AI content detectors (GPTZero, Originality.ai, Turnitin) and research
detectors (DetectGPT, Fast-DetectGPT, Binoculars) achieve high recall on
in-distribution machine-generated text but suffer from systematically elevated
false positive rates on specific human-authored subpopulations — non-native
English writing, neurodivergent prose, highly templatic genres (technical
documentation, legal filings, structured reviews), and text that has undergone
light post-processing (e.g., grammar correction tools). Reported false positive
rates on these groups range from15–40% in independent audits, producing real
harm: false accusations of academic dishonesty, demonetization of legitimate
creators, and erosion of trust in detection tools. The core challenge is not
merely increasing classifier sensitivity to machine text, but doing so without
sacrificing specificity on the long tail of legitimate human variation.

Hypothesis

A multi-stage detection pipeline that (i) calibrates confidence scores using a
linguistically-stratified human validation set, (ii) replaces hard binary
thresholds with conformal prediction sets that abstain on ambiguous inputs, and
(iii) explicitly models distribution shift between training and deployment human
text can reduce false positives by ≥50% relative to current single-threshold
detectors while maintaining recall ≥90% at a calibrated operating point. The
central claim is that false positives arise primarily from a mismatch between
the narrow human-language distribution seen during training and the broader
distribution encountered at inference — and that explicit modeling of this
mismatch is more effective than simply tuning the detection threshold.

Method

The framework operates in three sequential stages, with shared feature
extraction:

Stage1 — Feature Extraction. For each input text, compute four features from a
frozen surrogate language model (LLaMA-2-7B, no fine-tuning): (a) mean per-token
log-probability (surprisal), (b) per-sentence log-probability variance
(burstiness), (c) rank of the true token in the model's top-k distribution
averaged across positions (log-rank), and (d) the ratio of low-probability
tokens (<0.01) to total tokens (tail-ratio). These four features form a4-d
embedding.

Stage2 — Uncertainty-Aware Classification with Conformal Prediction. Train a
lightweight classifier (gradient-boosted trees, not a neural network — to
preserve interpretability and avoid distribution shift between training and
inference) on the4-d embeddings of a mixed human–machine corpus. Rather than
outputting a single label, the classifier produces a conformal prediction set:
for a user-specified coverage level (e.g.,90%), the set either contains exactly
one label ("Human" or "Machine"), both labels (ambiguous — abstain), or neither
(OOD — flag for manual review). The conformal calibration is performed on a
linguistically-stratified holdout set that oversamples the high-FPR
subpopulations listed in the problem statement.

Stage3 — Distribution-Shift Detection. For inputs where the prediction set
contains a single label but the classifier's raw confidence is below a secondary
threshold (chosen to maximize precision on a validation shift set), compute the
Mahalanobis distance from the input's4-d embedding to the training human-class
embedding centroid. If this distance exceeds the95th percentile of distances
observed in the calibration set, the input is downgraded from a binary
prediction to "abstain — likely distribution shift." This catches human text
that lies far from the training distribution without requiring retraining on
every new domain.

Training data. Use HC3 (human–ChatGPT comparison) as the primary training corpus
for the human and machine classes. Augment with M4 (multi-model, multi-domain
machine text) to broaden the machine class to include outputs from GPT-4,
Claude, Llama, and Gemini across five domains (news, creative writing, code,
scientific abstracts, social media). The human side is augmented with the TOEFL
essay corpus (non-native English) and Amazon review data (templatic writing) to
explicitly cover high-FPR subpopulations. Final training set: ~500K examples
(250K human,250K machine, balanced). Calibration set:50K examples stratified by
linguistically-defined subgroups (native, non-native, templatic, creative,
technical).

Dataset and Benchmark

Evaluate on RAID (Robust AI Detection benchmark,11 domains ×11 model families ×
multiple adversarial perturbations), supplemented with a constructed Adversarial
Human Corpus of5,000 human-written texts specifically designed to resemble
machine output (e.g., text written to a formulaic prompt, repetitive structures,
very low surprisal). Also construct a Coverage Stress Set from existing human
corpora:1,000 texts from each of four high-FPR subpopulations (non-native
English essays from TOEFL, autistic-authored blog text, templatic Amazon
reviews, and highly edited academic prose).

Expected Failure Modes

 1 Adversarial human text. A speaker deliberately mimicking machine-like
   patterns (high fluency, low variance, predictable token choices) may trigger
   the distribution-shift detector in Stage3 or fall inside the machine
   conformal set. This is an inherent limitation of any detector that relies on
   statistical signatures — we do not claim adversarial hardness, only
   robustness against natural distribution shift.
 2 Domain shift beyond calibration scope. If deployment text comes from an
   unobserved domain (e.g., low-resource language poetry, highly specialized
   medical jargon), both the conformal sets and the Mahalanobis distance may
   fail. The framework is designed to detect this failure (via large conformal
   sets or OOD flags) rather than handle it gracefully.
 3 Calibration set contamination. If the linguistically-stratified calibration
   set is too small per stratum (<500 examples per subgroup), subgroup-specific
   coverage may be mis-estimated, leading to uneven FPR reduction across groups.
 4 Feature saturation. The four features (surprisal, burstiness, log-rank,
   tail-ratio) are well-studied but may be jointly insufficient for a
   low-dimensional separator as detection models continue to improve. The
   framework can accept additional features without architectural changes, but
   this is not tested here.

Limitations

 • English-only evaluation. All training and test corpora are English.
   Multilingual generalization is not claimed and would require re-calibration
   on per-language conformal sets.
 • Fixed surrogate model. LLaMA-2-7B is used for feature extraction; a different
   surrogate may shift the feature space and require recalibration. The
   framework is not surrogate-agnostic.
 • No adversarial training. The detection pipeline is static — it does not
   incorporate adversarial examples during training. A motivated adversary with
   access to the detection pipeline could craft bypasses.
 • Synthetic data realism gap. The M4 corpus provides broad model coverage but
   is generated from fixed prompts; real-world machine text may include
   iterative human editing, partial regeneration, and hybrid authorship that the
   training distribution does not capture.
 • Computational cost ceiling. Conformal prediction requires a held calibration
   set and per-input forward passes through a language model (Stage1) plus tree
   ensemble inference (Stage2). Latency per input is ~2–5 seconds on consumer
   hardware, which may be prohibitive for real-time classroom proctoring but
   acceptable for asynchronous batch screening.
 • Abstain cost. The framework prefers abstention over confident error, which
   means operators receive "no decision" on up to15% of inputs. In deployment
   contexts that require a binary decision for every input, the abstain flag
   transfers the burden to human reviewers rather than eliminating it.
