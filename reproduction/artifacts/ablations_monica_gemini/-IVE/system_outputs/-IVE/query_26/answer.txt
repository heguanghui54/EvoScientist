A Proposal for Domain-Specific Data Filtering via Curriculum-Aware Selection##
  TitleCurriculum-Aware Data Filtering for Domain-Specific Language Models: A
                      Multi-Criterion Selection Framework

ProblemDomain-specific language models (legal, medical, financial, scientific)
require training data that is both domain-relevant and linguistically diverse.
Current filtering pipelines rely on heuristic rules (keyword matching,
perplexity thresholds) or single-criterion classifiers (domain classifier score
>0.9). These approaches suffer from two problems: (1) they discard hard but
valuable examples that the model needs to learn from late in training, and (2)
they retain easy, repetitive examples that saturate early and contribute no
further signal. The result is models that plateau early and generalize poorly to
the tail of the domain distribution.

HypothesisA curriculum-aware data filter — one that adapts the retained subset
to the model's current learning state across multiple quality axes (domain
relevance, lexical diversity, information density, and difficulty) — yields
higher downstream task performance than any single-criterion static filter,
because the optimal filtering policy is non-stationary over the course of
training.

MethodWe propose CurriFilter, a three-stage pipeline:

 1 Multi-criterion scoring. Each candidate document receives four scores:

 • Domain relevance: cosine similarity to a domain centroid computed from a
   small seed corpus of expert-curated domain documents.
 • Lexical diversity: type-token ratio and moving-average surprisal from a small
   reference LM.
 • Information density: TF-IDF entropy normalized by document length (penalizes
   boilerplate).
 • Difficulty: per-token cross-entropy under a small general-domain LM (higher =
   harder, i.e., more to learn).

 2 Curriculum-stage assignment. Documents are bucketed into three stages—early
   (high relevance, low difficulty), middle (balanced), late (high difficulty,
   high information density)—via k-means on the four-dimensional score vector.
 3 Adaptive sampling. At each training step, the sampling distribution over
   stages shifts from early-dominated to late-dominated using a linear decay
   schedule controlled by training progress ratio. Within each stage, documents
   are sampled proportional to their information density score.

Dataset / Benchmark- Pre-training corpus: A50 GB slice of the Pile's PubMed
Central subset (biomedical domain) as the candidate pool.

 • Downstream evaluation: - PubMedQA (QA)
 • BioASQ Task B (multi-label classification)
 • CHEMPROT (relation extraction)
 • A held-out50-document perplexity set from recent PubMed articles (temporal
   generalization)

Evaluation Metrics- Primary: Average downstream F1 across the three supervised
tasks.

 • Secondary: Held-out perplexity (domain-adapted), training efficiency
   (perplexity per token seen), and filter retention rate (fraction of corpus
   retained vs. performance).
 • Statistical rigor:3 seeds per condition;95% confidence intervals reported via
   bootstrap; Bonferroni correction applied when comparing across the four
   ablations.

Baselines1. No filter — train on the full50 GB corpus.

 2 Domain classifier — keep documents scoring above0.9 from a BERT-based PubMed
   classifier.
 3 Perplexity threshold — keep documents below the80th-percentile perplexity
   under GPT-2.
 4 D4 (recent work) — the diversity-aware filtering baseline from Xie et al.
   (2023), which selects for maximal n-gram coverage.

AblationsThe following ablations isolate each component's contribution:


 Ablation      Setting                         Expected delta from full model
 –Curriculum   Uniform sampling across stages  Small degradation on hard tasks
               (no curriculum)
 –Diversity    Drop lexical diversity score    Higher perplexity, retained set
                                               is repetitive
 –Difficulty   Drop difficulty score, stage    Worse late-stage learning,
               assignment uses3 axes           plateau
 –Static       Single fixed stage assignment   Worse than full, better than no
               (no schedule)                   filter

               regardless of scores


Expected Failure Modes- Score calibration drift: As training progresses, model
perplexity on all documents drops, potentially compressing the difficulty score
range and reducing stage separability. The k-means bucketing may need periodic
re-fit.

 • Domain centroid bias: The seed corpus may not cover the full domain, causing
   the relevance score to reject legitimate sub-topics. The centroid could be
   updated iteratively (future work).
 • Computation overhead: Four scoring passes over50 GB adds significant
   pre-processing cost. If this exceeds reasonable budgets, a learned
   approximator (e.g., a small MLP) could be distilled from scores on a5 GB
   sample.
 • Curriculum schedule sensitivity: The linear decay schedule is a strong
   assumption. If optimal stage switching is non-linear (e.g., sudden shift at a
   critical loss threshold), performance will degrade and a learned scheduler
   would be required.

Limitations- The four criteria are a reasonable but not exhaustive set. Semantic
coherence, factuality, and instruction-following suitability are not captured.
Expanding to6–8 criteria could improve coverage but adds engineering complexity.

 • The curriculum schedule is fixed a priori. Dynamic scheduling driven by
   validation loss plateaus is likely superior but introduces a
   meta-hyperparameter (patience threshold) that interacts with the filter.
 • Generalizability beyond the biomedical domain is untested. Legal or code
   domains may exhibit different optimal curricula (e.g., code may benefit from
   difficulty-first rather than easy-first).
 • The approach assumes token-level cross-entropy is a reasonable proxy for
   example difficulty. For autoregressive LMs this holds, but for encoder-only
   models or contrastive objectives the definition of difficulty is less clear.
 • Scaling: the four-score pipeline may not trivially extend to trillion-token
   web-scale corpora without approximate nearest-neighbor or reservoir-sampling
   shortcuts, which could introduce their own biases.
