 AI Agentic Workflow for Clinical Decision Support: A Multi-Agent Framework for
Differential Diagnosis and Treatment Planning## ProblemClinical decision-making
  in complex, multi-morbidity cases remains a high-error domain. Single large
language models used as standalone decision aids exhibit well-documented issues:
hallucination in diagnostic reasoning, sensitivity to prompt phrasing, inability
  to self-correct, and poor calibration of uncertainty. While LLM-as-judge and
chain-of-thought prompting improve structured reasoning, they do not provide the
 iterative verification, role specialization, or consensus-building mechanisms
 that characterize high-reliability human clinical teams. Existing multi-agent
healthcare frameworks either hardcode agent roles (preventing adaptation to case
complexity) or lack rigorous mechanisms for resolving inter-agent disagreement.
 No current system combines (a) role-specialized agents with distinct epistemic
 perspectives, (b) a structured deliberation protocol for conflict resolution,
 and (c) grounding in external clinical knowledge bases — all within a single,
                       reproducible evaluation pipeline.

HypothesisA multi-agent framework composed of specialized clinical reasoning
agents — each assigned a distinct epistemic role (diagnostician, evidence
reviewer, treatment planner, skeptic) — communicating via a structured
deliberation protocol will produce differential diagnoses and treatment plans
that are more accurate, more complete, and better calibrated than (1) a single
LLM with chain-of-thought prompting, (2) an ensemble of LLMs with majority
voting, and (3) a single LLM augmented with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)
from clinical literature. Specifically, we hypothesize that the skeptic agent —
tasked explicitly with identifying evidential gaps and alternative explanations
— will provide the largest marginal contribution to diagnostic accuracy among
all roles.

MethodArchitecture. A four-agent system operating over a shared case context:

 1 Diagnostician Agent — Proposes a differential diagnosis ranked by likelihood,
   with reasoning chains. Prompted to simulate inductive clinical reasoning from
   presented symptoms, history, and lab values.
 2 Evidence Reviewer Agent — Retrieves and summarizes relevant literature (via a
   frozen RAG pipeline over PubMed abstracts and clinical guidelines). Assesses
   each proposed diagnosis against available evidence.
 3 Treatment Planner Agent — Given a provisional diagnosis, proposes treatment
   options with rationale, contraindications, and monitoring recommendations.
 4 Skeptic Agent — Reviews the Diagnostician's differential and the Evidence
   Reviewer's assessment, identifies missing information, alternative
   explanations, cognitive biases, and requests specific clarifications or
   additional tests.

Deliberation Protocol (modified Delphi). All agents read the case independently.
The Diagnostician produces an initial differential. The Evidence Reviewer
annotates each candidate. The Skeptic issues critiques and queries. The
Diagnostician may revise. After up to three rounds, a final differential and
treatment plan are produced. Agreement is measured via inter-agent rank
correlation across rounds; persistent disagreement triggers an explicit
uncertainty flag in the output.

Grounding. The Evidence Reviewer uses a frozen RAG pipeline (BM25 +
Sentence-BERT retriever over MedQA-relevant PubMed abstracts,2020–2025). No
online search. No training or fine-tuning of the underlying LLM.

LLM Backend. GPT-4o for all agents (temperature0.3 for consistency;
temperature0.7 for the Skeptic to encourage divergent thinking).

Dataset / BenchmarkMEDQA (USMLE Step2 &3 subset) —1,273 multiple-choice
questions with clinical vignettes. We use the open-ended answer variant (no
options provided to the system; system generates a free-text diagnosis and we
match against gold-standard answers via semantic equivalence judged by an
independent LLM). This avoids the gaming of multiple-choice options.

MIMIC-IV (subsample) —200 de-identified emergency department cases with
confirmed discharge diagnoses. Used for open-ended differential diagnosis
generation. Cases are selected to include at least one co-morbidity and one
diagnostic uncertainty signal (e.g., equivocal lab values, overlapping symptom
profiles).

Primary metric per case: Top-1 diagnostic accuracy, Top-3 recall (whether the
correct diagnosis appears in the top three of the differential), and
completeness (proportion of the gold-standard workup steps mentioned in the
treatment plan).

Expected Failure Modes1. Agent homogenization. Despite distinct role prompts,
all four agents may converge to similar reasoning patterns because they share
the same base LLM. If inter-agent disagreement is low across most cases, the
deliberation protocol provides no benefit over a single model, and the skeptic
role is effectively vacuous. Detection: compute average pairwise rank
correlation of differentials across agents. Correlation >0.85 across all case
types would indicate homogenization.

 2 Skeptic over-rejection. The skeptic agent, prompted to be critical, may
   reject valid diagnoses too aggressively, reducing Top-3 recall even when the
   Diagnostician was initially correct. Detection: compare the skeptic's
   false-negative rate on cases where the Diagnostician's top candidate was
   correct.
 3 RAG retrieval brittleness. The frozen RAG pipeline may fail on cases
   requiring reasoning about rare diseases, very recent findings (post-2025), or
   non-textual data (imaging, lab trends over time). This would limit the
   Evidence Reviewer's utility to the most common conditions, systematically
   under-serving the cases where decision support is most needed. Detection:
   stratify Top-1 accuracy by case rarity (frequency in training corpus).
 4 Deliberation divergence. Rather than converging, agents may oscillate or
   drift across rounds, especially on ambiguous cases. The fixed three-round
   limit may cut off convergence prematurely or force convergence to a
   lower-quality consensus. Detection: measure cross-round change in
   differential rankings; flag cases where rank correlation between round2 and
   round3 is <0.7.
 5 Perturbation instability. Small rephrasings of the vignette may produce
   substantially different differentials. This is a known failure of LLM-based
   clinical reasoning and may be amplified rather than mitigated by multi-agent
   interaction if all agents share the same sensitivity pattern. Detection: the
   Perturbation Stability metric directly measures this; if <0.6 across the
   MIMIC subset, the system is not robust enough for deployment.

Limitations1. No image modalities. The system processes only text vignettes.
Real clinical decision support must integrate imaging, pathology, and waveform
data. Extending to multimodal inputs is left to future work.

 2 No online updating. The RAG corpus is static (2020–2025). Rapidly evolving
   areas (emerging pathogens, new drug approvals) are not reflected. A
   production system would require continuous index updates.
 3 Single LLM backend. All agents use GPT-4o. Results may not generalize to
   other base models (e.g., Claude, Gemini, open-weight models). The relative
   contributions of role specialization vs. base model capability cannot be
   disentangled without cross-model replication.
 4 Benchmark scope. MEDQA and MIMIC-IV cover US-based clinical practice.
   Diagnostic reasoning patterns, treatment guidelines, and prevalent conditions
   vary significantly across healthcare systems. Generalizability to non-US,
   low-resource, or primary-care settings is unvalidated.
 5 No safety-critical evaluation. Diagnostic accuracy on benchmarks is a proxy
   measure. Real-world deployment requires prospective clinical validation,
   assessment of workflow integration, and measurement of downstream patient
   outcomes. This proposal addresses only the algorithmic foundation.
 6 Evaluation via LLM judge. Using an LLM to judge semantic equivalence for
   open-ended diagnosis introduces circularity concerns. A secondary human
   evaluation on a100-case subset (with two board-certified physicians) is
   planned but not yet funded or IRB-approved.
 7 Calibration is measured, not controlled. The uncertainty flag rate is
   descriptive; the system cannot dynamically adjust its decision threshold or
   defer to human review based on estimated confidence. A calibrated deferral
   mechanism is a natural extension but is not implemented here.
