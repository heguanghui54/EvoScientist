Learned Adaptive Draft Horizon for Speculative Decoding in Large Language
Models### ProblemSpeculative decoding reduces inference latency by having a
small draft model propose K tokens per step while the target model verifies them
in parallel via a single forward pass. Every implementation to date uses a
fixed, predetermined draft length K (e.g., K=4 or K=5). This is substantially
suboptimal: when the target model is highly confident and would accept most or
all draft tokens, the fixed horizon underutilizes parallelism and leaves latency
on the table; when the target model would reject many draft tokens, a long
horizon wastes compute on tokens that will be discarded, and the effective
acceptance rate collapses. Since the difficulty of predicting the next token
varies widely across decoding steps (a boilerplate "Sure, here is" sequence is
far easier than an unexpected noun), a fixed horizon must compromise between
these regimes, and no single K achieves optimal throughput across positions,
prompts, or domains.

HypothesisA lightweight learned critic that predicts the optimal draft length
for the next speculative step — conditioned on features drawn from the target
model's recent hidden states — can reduce mean per-token latency by at least20%
relative to any fixed-horizon baseline, without degrading generation quality
(measured by perplexity and task accuracy). The critic exploits the observation
that autoregressive hidden states encode difficulty: when the target model's
hidden states are stable and near-deterministic across recent positions, longer
drafts are safe; when they shift abruptly, shorter drafts avoid wasted compute.
