Table3 – Ablation Variant (-IDE): Complete Research Proposal---

TitleCalibrated Consistency: A Multi-Pass Agreement Framework for Accurate
Automated LLM Evaluation

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ProblemAutomated evaluation of LLM outputs using LLM-as-a-judge methods suffers
from systematic inaccuracies: position bias, verbosity bias, self-enhancement
bias (judges favoring their own outputs), and poor calibration on nuanced
quality dimensions (coherence, factual consistency, instruction following).
Current single-pass scoring or pairwise comparison protocols produce high
variance and low inter-rater reliability compared to human judgments. These
issues compound when automated evaluation is used for model development — noisy
signals lead to suboptimal training decisions and unreliable leaderboard
rankings.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

HypothesisA multi-pass agreement framework that aggregates multiple independent
evaluation passes with controlled randomization of prompt order, rubric
decomposition, and judge model diversity will reduce bias and variance,
achieving Pearson/Spearman correlations with human judgments that exceed both
single-pass LLM judges and standard baseline methods. Specifically, decomposing
a single holistic quality score into3–5 factor-specific sub-scores (e.g.,
factuality, coherence, instruction following, verbosity penalty) and aggregating
across (k\geq3) structurally diverse judge models will outperform any
single-judge setup.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Method### Stage1: Multi-Pass Eval ProtocolFor each (model, prompt, reference
response) triple:

 1 Rubric decomposition – Replace a single holistic score with4 sub-criteria
   scored on a1–5 Likert scale:

 • Factual consistency: Does the output contradict known facts or provided
   context?
 • Instruction fidelity: Does the output follow all constraints in the prompt?
 • Coherence & structure: Is the argument logically ordered and well-formed?
 • Conciseness: Does the output avoid unnecessary verbosity? (penalty for
   over-generation)

 2 Judge model diversity – Use3 structurally different judge models (e.g.,
   Mixtral-8x7B, GPT-4o-mini, Llama-3-70B) to reduce systematic bias.
 3 Randomized presentation – For each judge model, run2 passes with candidate
   order shuffled (when pairwise) or candidate presentation order randomized
   (when scoring). Aggregate via trimmed mean (discard highest and lowest per
   dimension).
 4 Calibration layer – Fit a linear regression or isotonic regression on a
   held-out human-annotated set (N=500) mapping aggregate judge scores to human
   scores, producing recalibrated final scores per dimension.

Stage2: Consensus ScoringCompute inter-judge agreement (Krippendorff's alpha
across the3 judge models ×2 passes =6 judgments per dimension). Flag cases with
alpha <0.4 for manual review. For non-flagged items, use the mean of all6
recalibrated scores. Report both the calibrated score and the agreement
statistic alongside it.

Stage3: Uncertainty ReportingInstead of a single point estimate, report
per-example evaluation results as a tuple: (mean score,95% CI from6 judgments,
agreement α). For benchmark-level metrics, report the distribution of CIs and
the proportion of items exceeding the agreement threshold.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Dataset / BenchmarkPrimary: MT-Bench (80 multi-turn questions,3.3K human
preference judgments labeled by domain experts on a1–10 scale across6 models).
This is the most widely used benchmark for LLM judge accuracy and provides
published human baselines.

Secondary: Chatbot Arena subset —1,000 curated conversations with human Elo
ratings (released as a public dataset) to test generalization beyond the
MT-Bench format.

Calibration set: A randomly sampled20% of the MT-Bench human annotations (N=660
judgments) will be held out for fitting the calibration regression.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Evaluation Metrics| Metric | Rationale |

|---|---| | Spearman ρ (primary) | Rank-order correlation with human scores;
captures whether the evaluator preserves relative ordering | | Pearson r |
Linear correlation with human scores; captures absolute agreement | |
Krippendorff's α | Inter-judge reliability among the6 judge passes (ordinal
weighting) | | Expected Calibration Error (ECE) | Binned calibration error after
recalibration — how well do predicted scores match human score distribution? | |
Flag rate | Proportion of items with α <0.4 requiring manual review (lower is
better; controlled human cost) |

All metrics reported with bootstrap95% CIs (10,000 resamples).

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Baselines1. LLM-only single-pass (GPT-4) — Standard single LLM judge with no
rubric, no randomization (state-of-the-art baseline).

 2 Pairwise Elo — Bradley-Terry Elo computed from pairwise comparisons (Chatbot
   Arena protocol, single judge model).
 3 Rubric-only — Single judge model with rubric decomposition but no multi-pass
   aggregation.
 4 Multi-pass single model —6 passes (2× with randomized order) from one judge
   model, no diversity.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Expected Failure Modes1. Calibration overfitting — The regression layer may
overfit to the specific distribution of MT-Bench human scores, failing to
generalize to the Chatbot Arena subset. Mitigation: evaluate cross-benchmark
transfer and report per-benchmark performance separately.

 2 Diminishing returns on judge count — Three structurally diverse judges may
   not be enough; five may be needed for stable agreement thresholds.
   Mitigation: include an exploratory analysis using5 judges on a subset (200
   items) to confirm the saturation point.
 3 Rubric overspecification — Decomposing into4 sub-scores may miss emergent
   quality dimensions (e.g., creativity, safety) that humans consider
   holistically. Mitigation: include an open-ended "overall impression"
   sub-score as a fifth dimension and compare against the4-factor version.
 4 Agreement filter bias — Items with low inter-judge agreement (α <0.4) may be
   systematically harder or more ambiguous cases, not just noise. Excluding them
   inflates apparent accuracy. Mitigation: report results both with and without
   flagged items; analyze the flagged subset qualitatively.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Limitations1. Judge model access — GPT-4o-mini and Llama-3-70B are accessible
now, but closed-source judge models create reproducibility dependencies. The
Mixtral-8x7B component is fully open-weight and replicable.

 2 Benchmark coverage — MT-Bench and Chatbot Arena cover instruction-following
   and conversation quality but not domain-specific evaluation (medical, legal,
   code generation). The method's generality to specialized domains is untested.
 3 Calibration cost — The calibration set requires500+ human annotations per new
   benchmark, which may be prohibitive for rapid prototyping. Active learning or
   few-shot calibration is left for future work.
 4 Throughput —6 LLM API calls per evaluated item ×4 sub-scores increases
   evaluation cost by6× over a single-pass judge. The trade-off between cost and
   accuracy is characterized but not optimized.
 5 No meta-evaluation — This proposal evaluates the evaluator against human
   judgments, but does not address whether improved evaluation fidelity
   translates into better downstream model training (e.g., RLHF reward signal
   quality). That is a separate evaluation loop not covered here.
