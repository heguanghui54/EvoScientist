# Morphology-Aware Translation from Scratch: When Simple Segmentation Beats Large Pretrained Models for Low-Resource Machine Translation

Short Hypothesis: For extremely low-resource language pairs (e.g., <10k sentence pairs), training a small Transformer from scratch with explicit morphological segmentation (e.g., using Morfessor) can achieve higher translation quality than fine-tuning a large pretrained multilingual model (e.g., mBART). The rationale is that large pretrained models overfit to tiny data, while morphological segmentation reduces vocabulary sparsity and improves word alignment, allowing a smaller model to learn more effectively. This setting is ideal because it directly challenges the dominant paradigm of 'bigger is better' in low-resource MT, and the comparison is fair and controlled.

Related Work: Existing low-resource MT heavily relies on multilingual pretraining (e.g., mBART, M2M-100) or data augmentation (back-translation, self-training). Morphological segmentation has been used as a preprocessing step (Sennrich et al., 2016; Ataman et al., 2017), but it has not been systematically compared against large pretrained models in extremely low-resource settings. Our proposal uniquely tests whether a simple, interpretable, from-scratch approach can outperform resource-intensive pretrained models, challenging the assumption that transfer learning is always superior. This is not a trivial extension because it requires careful control of model size, data, and segmentation methods.

Abstract: Low-resource machine translation (MT) remains a challenge, with state-of-the-art approaches relying on large multilingual pretrained models or extensive data augmentation. However, these methods often require significant computational resources and may not be optimal for truly low-resource languages with rich morphology. We hypothesize that for extremely low-resource language pairs (e.g., <10k parallel sentences), training a small Transformer from scratch using explicit morphological segmentation (e.g., Morfessor) can outperform fine-tuning a large pretrained multilingual model (e.g., mBART). We propose a systematic comparison across multiple low-resource language pairs with varying morphological complexity (Turkish-English, Swahili-English, Finnish-English). We evaluate using BLEU, chrF++, and human evaluation of fluency and adequacy. Our experiments also investigate the effect of model size, segmentation granularity, and data amount. We anticipate that the morphology-aware from-scratch approach will yield competitive or superior performance, especially for morphologically rich target languages. This work challenges the prevailing trend of relying on large pretrained models and offers a simpler, more interpretable alternative for extremely low-resource scenarios.

Experiments: 1. Data: Use three low-resource language pairs: Turkish-English (SETIMES, <10k sentences), Swahili-English (JW300, filtered to <10k), Finnish-English (Europarl, filtered to <10k). Include a higher-resource control (100k sentences) for scaling analysis. 2. Models: (a) Baseline: Small Transformer (6 layers, 512 hidden, 8 heads) trained from scratch with BPE (16k merges). (b) Morphology-aware: Same Transformer but with Morfessor segmentation (trained on target monolingual data). (c) Large pretrained: Fine-tune mBART-50 (or M2M-100-418M) on the same data. (d) Ablation: BPE with larger vocabulary (32k) to control for vocabulary size. 3. Training: From-scratch models trained for 100k steps (Adam, label smoothing, early stopping). Fine-tuning for 20k steps. 4. Metrics: BLEU (sacrébleu), chrF++, human evaluation (fluency and adequacy on 1-5 scale for 200 sentences per pair). 5. Analysis: Vocabulary coverage, OOV rate, alignment quality (fast_align), training time, GPU memory.

Risk Factors and Limitations: - Hypothesis may not hold for all language pairs; some may still benefit from pretrained models. - Morfessor segmentation may not be optimal for all languages (e.g., non-agglutinative). - Model size mismatch could confound results; we control by scaling small transformer. - Human evaluation is costly and subjective; we use crowdsourcing with quality controls. - Results may not generalize to extremely low-resource languages without any monolingual data for Morfessor training.
