# Adversarial Preference Refinement: Learning Human Values Through Active Query Generation

Short Hypothesis: Current value alignment methods (RLHF, IRL) assume human preferences are static and can be elicited through passive queries. We hypothesize that human values are often latent and inconsistent, and that an AI agent can more efficiently learn aligned values by actively generating adversarial preference queries—edge cases designed to expose contradictions and refine the human's own preference model. This contrasts with standard approaches that treat the human as an oracle with fixed preferences.

Related Work: Standard RLHF (Christiano et al., 2017) learns a reward model from passive human comparisons. Inverse reinforcement learning (Ng & Russell, 2000) assumes an expert demonstrates optimal behavior. Active learning for preferences (Settles, 2009) focuses on query efficiency but not on deliberately exposing human inconsistencies. Work on 'reward hacking' (Amodei et al., 2016) and 'specification gaming' (Krakovna et al., 2020) shows that agents exploit misspecified rewards, but no prior work uses the agent to actively challenge the human's own preference model to improve alignment. Our proposal is the first to treat the human's preference system as a moving target that can be improved through adversarial interaction, rather than a static source of ground truth.

Abstract: Value alignment—ensuring AI systems act in accordance with human preferences—remains a central challenge. Current methods like reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) treat human preferences as a static oracle, passively collecting comparisons to learn a reward model. However, humans often hold inconsistent, incomplete, or poorly articulated values, especially in complex or unfamiliar scenarios. We propose Adversarial Preference Refinement (APR), a novel framework where an AI agent actively generates preference queries designed to expose contradictions in the human's stated preferences. By learning to generate trajectories that maximize disagreement with the current reward model, the agent helps the human recognize and resolve inconsistencies in their own values. This iterative process refines both the human's preference model and the agent's reward model simultaneously. We hypothesize that this adversarial co-adaptation leads to more robust alignment than passive data collection. We test APR in two environments: a resource allocation task where humans must balance efficiency and fairness, and a text generation task where humans must specify nuanced content policies. We compare against standard RLHF, active preference learning, and reward model ensembling. Our primary metric is alignment robustness: the agent's ability to avoid reward hacking and maintain high-quality behavior under distribution shift. We also measure human satisfaction and cognitive load. APR offers a new paradigm for alignment that embraces the dynamic, constructive nature of human values.

Experiments: We will conduct two main experiments:

**Experiment 1: Resource Allocation Game.** We build a 2D gridworld where an agent allocates resources to simulated 'citizens' with varying needs. The human overseer provides pairwise comparisons of allocation trajectories. The APR agent, after initial reward model training, generates adversarial queries: it searches for trajectories that are nearly equally preferred by the current reward model but are maximally different in terms of fairness vs efficiency trade-offs (e.g., giving all resources to one agent vs. equal distribution). The human compares these trajectories, often revealing that they dislike extreme solutions or find them inconsistent with earlier preferences. We measure: (a) reduction in reward model uncertainty on adversarial examples, (b) alignment robustness: we introduce a distribution shift (e.g., a new citizen with urgent need) and measure if the agent maintains appropriate behavior, (c) number of queries needed to reach a stable preference model.

**Experiment 2: Text Content Policy Refinement.** We use a language model (e.g., GPT-2) to generate assistant responses. The human specifies a content policy (e.g., 'be helpful but avoid giving medical advice'). The APR agent generates responses that probe the boundary of this policy (e.g., a response that gives very cautious medical advice, or a response that is unhelpful but avoids medical topics). The human compares these against safer alternatives, forcing them to clarify their policy. Metrics: (a) consistency of human preferences over time (measured by test-retest reliability), (b) reduction in reward model uncertainty, (c) human-rated satisfaction with final policy clarity.

**Baselines:** Standard RLHF (passive random queries), active learning (uncertainty sampling), and reward model ensemble (using disagreement between multiple models).

**Evaluation Metrics:** (1) Reward model calibration error, (2) Alignment robustness (performance under distribution shift without reward hacking), (3) Human cognitive load (time taken per query, self-reported difficulty), (4) Preference consistency (test-retest correlation).

Risk Factors and Limitations: 1. **Human cognitive load:** Adversarial queries may confuse or frustrate humans, leading to lower quality feedback. We mitigate by limiting adversarial difficulty and providing explanations. 2. **Adversarial search complexity:** Finding maximally informative queries could be computationally expensive. We use approximate methods (e.g., deep Q-networks to generate candidate trajectories). 3. **Convergence:** The co-adaptive process may not converge if the human keeps changing preferences. We monitor for cyclic behavior and implement a stopping criterion (e.g., when reward model uncertainty stabilizes). 4. **Generalizability:** Results in simple gridworlds and text tasks may not transfer to real-world high-stakes alignment. We discuss this limitation explicitly. 5. **Human subjects:** We assume cooperative, thoughtful human participants; results may differ with unengaged users. We will recruit motivated participants and provide training. 6. **Ethical concern:** The agent could manipulate human preferences rather than align to them. We design queries to expose inconsistencies, not to persuade, and we measure human satisfaction to ensure autonomy is respected.
